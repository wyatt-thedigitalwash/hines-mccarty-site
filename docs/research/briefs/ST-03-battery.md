**`784.03 1A1 domestic battery` is the query that tests the boundary, and the
honest answer splits it.** The statute number is this page's: F.S.
784.03(1)(a)1. is quoted here and nowhere else on the site. The word "domestic"
is the DV page's. The searcher typing that string is looking for a statute that
does not exist, because **there is no domestic battery statute in Florida.** A
domestic battery is a 784.03 battery charged where the parties fall inside the
F.S. 741.28(3) relationship definition. That correction is one sentence, it
belongs on this page because this page is where 784.03 is quoted, and it ends
with a link to the DV page. It is not developed here. That single sentence is the
entire domestic content of this page.

### The one row that appears on both pages, and why that is correct

| Statute | Conduct | Relationship required | Degree | Offense level |

This page carries 784.031 as its own offense and names 784.041(2) in one table

### 3.3 The repeat question, and how it is handled

**"Can battery charges be dropped in Florida?" appears in both PAA sets on the
same day.** It is the only question in this project's records to surface on both
the commercial and the informational SERP for the same charge. That is a stronger
demand signal than any single-set appearance, and it matches this brief's
charge-specific direction, which flagged it as the highest-intent question here.

**Disposition: FAQ, answered strictly as process and prosecutorial discretion.**

The factual answer is verified and quoted in Section 7: **F.S. 27.02(1)** provides
that "The state attorney shall appear in the circuit and county courts within his
or her judicial circuit and prosecute or defend on behalf of the state all suits,
applications, or motions, civil or criminal, **in which the state is a party**."
The person who was allegedly battered is a witness. The State is the party. The
decision to file, to reduce, or to drop is the State Attorney's.

**Compliance rails on this answer, and they are strict:**

- No prediction, no likelihood, no rate, no "often," no "many cases."
- No statement about what this firm can obtain. Rule 4-7.13(b) results
  characterisation.
- No implication that a complainant's affidavit of non-prosecution ends a case.
  That is the misconception, and correcting it is the value.
- The answer states who decides. It does not state what they will decide.

**Where the DV boundary bites, and it bites here hardest.** The DV page answers
the same underlying question from **F.S. 741.2901(2)**, the pro-prosecution
mandate, which is its C9. This page answers it from **F.S. 27.02(1)**, the
general duty. Two statutes, two answers, no shared sentence. The DV page's answer
is stronger and more specific and it is scoped to domestic cases. This page's is
the general rule. **Do not import 741.2901 into this page's answer.** If the
reader's case is domestic, the link takes them to the page where the stronger
answer lives.

**One honest flag on rule 11A.2 rule 1.** "Can battery charges be dropped" is not
strictly nonsensical about another charge, so it does not pass rule 1 cleanly in
isolation. Rule 5 does not bar it: it has surfaced on no other charge SERP in
this project's records, unlike the 33-day question. It is kept because the answer

### 3.4 The bail question, and what was actually found

"How much is bail for a battery charge in Florida?" was expected to be
unanswerable. It is answerable, from the circuit's own published administrative
order, and the answer is more interesting than a number.

Verified and quoted in Section 7 from **Administrative Order S-2025-063**,
Thirteenth Judicial Circuit, effective **January 1, 2026 through December 31,
2026**:

1. The circuit adopted **the uniform statewide bond schedule adopted by the
   Florida Supreme Court, without change.**
2. **"This bond schedule applies only to the booking officer, not to judges. This
   schedule does not bind any judge conducting first appearance hearings or bail
   bond hearings."**
3. The schedule sets **$1,000** for "First degree misdemeanor ... that involves
   any amount of force or threat of force against a person" and **$5,000** for
   "Third degree felony that involves any amount of force or threat of force
   against a person."
4. **And a list of offenses to which the schedule does not apply at all**, where
   the person may not be released before first appearance. That list names
   "felony battery; domestic battery by strangulation; ... assault or battery on
   a law enforcement officer; ... assault or battery on a person 65 years of age
   or older," and separately "A capital felony, life felony, felony of the first
   degree, or felony of the second degree," which reaches aggravated battery.

**So the honest answer to the PAA question is: for several battery offenses there
is no bond schedule amount, because the schedule does not apply and the person is
held for a judge.** That is a better answer than a number, it is local, it is
dated, it is current for 2026, and no page in either SERP has it.

**Constraint on how this ships.** The schedule rows and the exclusion list are
verified quotations. **Mapping a particular battery charge onto a particular row
is an application of the order, not a quotation of it**, and it sits on the far
side of the plan Section 10.3 allowlist in the same way ST-01's 921.0023 defaults
did. The page presents the two verified pieces, the schedule and the carve-out,
and says which offenses the order itself names. It does not print "bail for
battery is $1,000."

## 6. The C9 differentiator

### 6.1 The instruction this brief was given, and what the statutes actually support

The charge-specific direction proposed a structural fact: that battery is the
offense where the identity of the victim and the defendant's own record both
reclassify the same physical act, and that this is unusual in a project set whose
Florida ladders otherwise escalate on an amount.

**Both halves were tested against the operative text. The first half holds. The
second half does not hold as cleanly as stated, and this brief says so rather
than stretching the text to fit.**

**What holds, verified.** F.S. 784.03(2), 784.07(2)(b), and 784.08(2)(c) each
take the identical first degree misdemeanour battery and make it a third degree
felony, on the defendant's record in the first case and on the victim's identity
in the other two. Quoted in Section 7.

**What does not hold.** "Reclassification on the defendant's own record is
unusual in this set" is **false**. F.S. 812.014(3)(b) and (3)(c) do the same
thing for theft: a petit theft with one prior theft conviction is a first degree
misdemeanour, and with two or more it is a third degree felony. Fetched and
quoted in Section 7 specifically to test this claim. Building the C9 on
"prior-conviction reclassification is unique to battery" would have shipped a
false differentiator on a page whose sibling is the grand theft page.

**What survives the test, and it is sharper than the original idea.** Inside
chapter 784, the three routes do not work alike, and the difference is a mental
state:

- **784.03(2)** needs **one** prior conviction, and the subsection defines
  "conviction" to include a determination of guilt "regardless of whether
  adjudication is withheld or a plea of nolo contendere is entered."
  812.014(3)(c) needs **two or more** and contains no such definition.
- **784.07(2)** applies only where a person is "charged with **knowingly**
  committing an assault or battery upon a law enforcement officer" and the others
  listed.
- **784.08(2)** applies "**regardless of whether he or she knows or has reason to
  know the age of the victim**."

Two victim-identity reclassifications, sitting one section apart, producing the
same degree and the same offense level, and one requires the defendant to know
who the victim was while the other expressly does not. That is verified from the
operative text of both and it is true of exactly one page in this project.

### 6.2 The C9, in the required shape

```
statute:   "F.S. 784.03"
section:   "What raises a battery without changing what happened"
assertion: "The same first degree misdemeanor battery becomes a third degree felony on facts outside the act itself, and the three routes do not work alike: 784.03(2) needs only one prior battery conviction and counts a withheld adjudication or a nolo plea as that conviction, 784.07(2)(b) applies only where the defendant knowingly battered a law enforcement officer or other listed person, and 784.08(2)(c) reaches a person 65 years of age or older regardless of whether the defendant knows or has reason to know the age of the victim."
```

### 6.3 The cover-the-name test

Cover the word "battery" and read section 5 of the outline.

- Swap in **grand theft** and the sentence is false twice over. 812.014 escalates
  on the value of the property, from the $750 felony threshold to the $100,000
  first degree tier, and its prior-conviction route needs two priors and has no
  withhold clause. No victim-identity reclassification exists in it at all.
- Swap in **drug trafficking** and it becomes substance-specific weight tiers
  with mandatory minimums. Nobody is reclassified by who was standing there.
- Swap in **money laundering** and it becomes $300, $20,000, and $100,000 across
  a rolling 12 months.
- Swap in **unauthorized computer access** and it becomes a harm-consequence
  ladder reaching the first degree through human life and medical equipment.
- Swap in **domestic violence** and the sentence is not merely false, it is the
  wrong axis: the DV page turns on **who the other person is in relation to the
  defendant**, which is a relationship, while 784.07 and 784.08 turn on **what
  the other person is**, an officer or a person of an age, with no relationship
  to the defendant required or relevant.
- Swap in **identity theft** and see 6.4, because that one is close and deserves
  more than a line.

**The load-bearing clause is the knowledge asymmetry.** No other page in this set
contains two adjacent provisions where one demands knowledge of the victim's
status and the other disclaims it in terms. That sentence is true of exactly one
page.

### 6.6 The boundary, proved rather than asserted

### 7.7 F.S. 784.08, assault or battery on persons 65 years of age or older

**https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/784.08**

Section title, quoted:
> "Assault or battery on persons 65 years of age or older; reclassification of offenses; minimum sentence."

Full operative text, quoted:
> "(1) A person who is convicted of an aggravated assault or aggravated battery upon a person 65 years of age or older shall be sentenced to a minimum term of imprisonment of 3 years and fined not more than $10,000 and shall also be ordered by the sentencing judge to make restitution to the victim of such offense and to perform up to 500 hours of community service work. Restitution and community service work shall be in addition to any fine or sentence which may be imposed and shall not be in lieu thereof.
> (2) Whenever a person is charged with committing an assault or aggravated assault or a battery or aggravated battery upon a person 65 years of age or older, regardless of whether he or she knows or has reason to know the age of the victim, the offense for which the person is charged shall be reclassified as follows: (a) In the case of aggravated battery, from a felony of the second degree to a felony of the first degree. (b) In the case of aggravated assault, from a felony of the third degree to a felony of the second degree. (c) In the case of battery, from a misdemeanor of the first degree to a felony of the third degree. (d) In the case of assault, from a misdemeanor of the second degree to a misdemeanor of the first degree.
> (3) Notwithstanding the provisions of s. 948.01, adjudication of guilt or imposition of sentence shall not be suspended, deferred, or withheld."

**The mandatory minimum was checked from the operative text as instructed, and
the instruction was correct to require it.** The 3-year minimum in (1) attaches
**only to aggravated assault and aggravated battery** on a person 65 or older. A
simple battery reclassified to a third degree felony under **(2)(c) carries no
mandatory minimum at all.** The 921.0022 chart row reads only "Battery on a
person 65 years of age or older" and would not have shown that. **A page that
said "battery on a person over 65 carries a three-year minimum" would be wrong,
and it is the kind of wrong that plan Section 10.3 exists to prevent.**

**A second provision that matters more to a reader than the minimum does.**
784.08(3) bars a withhold of adjudication, and it is a standalone subsection
covering the section rather than a clause attached to (1). Every populated
assertion in this project's set is about degrees and amounts; **this is a
provision about what a court may not do at disposition, and it is the single most
consequential sentence on the page for a first-time defendant.** It is verified,
quotable, and absent from every competitor page graded.
