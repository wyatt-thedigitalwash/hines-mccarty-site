## Not said, because I could not source it

- The meaning of "transitory foreign substance". The statute does not define
  it, and the case law was not fetched. The page offers only a plain gloss (see
  attorney item 1).
- Whether a wet-floor sign or other warning defeats or reduces a claim (this
  is case law). Not on the page.
- Any duty on a business to keep video, or the law on destroyed evidence
  (spoliation). The page only advises asking for the video to be kept.
- Claims against a government body, including notice rules (768.28 is not
  recorded). The page says only that the trip and fall page covers falls on
  public property, and links there.
- Landlord, apartment and private-home premises duties. The page says only
  that a home falls outside the business rule and that other rules decide it.
- Federal reimbursement rules (self-funded employer health plans and Medicare
  liens), and the 30-day collateral-source notice process in 768.76(6) and (7).
- Which employers workers' compensation covers (440.02), its exceptions on
  fault (440.09(3)), and third-party claims by an injured worker (440.39).
- When a slip and fall claim "arises" (accrual). The page keys the limit to
  when the claim arose, as the car page does, and never says "the date of the
  fall".
- Whether the 2023 change to the fault bar reaches a given case by its filing
  date. That is s.30 of ch. 2023-15. The page states the current rule and does
  not give a date for it.
- Fees, case values and timelines.

## Items for attorney review

1. The gloss of "transitory foreign substance" as "something on the floor that
   does not belong there, like a spill". The statute does not define the term.
2. The workers' compensation card. It is new territory for these pages. Is the
   wording ("if your employer is covered", "generally", "narrow exceptions,
   such as an employer that failed to secure the coverage the law requires")
   right, and does this card belong on a slip and fall pillar at all?
3. The health-plan reimbursement wording. 768.76(4) applies only to a provider
   "that has complied with this section", and ERISA or other federal rules may
   override it for some plans. The page hedges with "may", "generally" and a
   send-to-a-lawyer line. Confirm that is enough.
4. "A fall at a private home is outside this rule, and other rules decide that
   kind of claim." This reads the statute's scope ("business establishment")
   and leaves the other rules unnamed.
5. The do-now and evidence advice: tracks, dirt and size as signs of how long
   a spill was there, a dripping machine as an example of a regular condition,
   and the written request to keep video. Confirm these fit how the firm
   handles these cases.
6. Who pays order. Florida law sets no payment order for premises claims, so
   the cards follow how a reader thinks about it: the business, its insurer,
   other parties at fault, your health plan, then workers' compensation.
7. The sibling links (trip-and-fall, brain-injuries, wrongful-death) and their
   labels ("Trip and fall claims, including falls on public property", "Brain
   injury claims and what they involve", "Wrongful death claims: who can bring
   one"). Confirm the finished sibling pages match these labels, especially
   that trip-and-fall really covers falls on public property.
