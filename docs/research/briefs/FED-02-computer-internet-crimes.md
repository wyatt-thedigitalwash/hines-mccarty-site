## 0. THE CONSTRAINT, AND THE VERDICT

**The constraint.** The shared core with F.S. 815.06 is unauthorized access, and
18 U.S.C. 1030's protected-computer requirement is largely an interstate
commerce hook. **The page must be built on 1030(a)(1), (a)(4), and (a)(5). If
only (a)(2) or (a)(3) support a page, report collapse.**

**Verdict: CONSTRAINT SATISFIED. The pair does not collapse.** All three named
subsections were fetched and all three carry elements. But the verdict is more
qualified than federal money laundering's, and the qualification matters for
whoever writes the page.

| Subsection | Element it adds | Distinctness from Florida |
|---|---|---|
| **(a)(1)** | National defense or foreign relations information, or restricted data under the Atomic Energy Act, plus willful communication or willful retention | **Total. F.S. 815.06 has no analogue of any kind.** This is the cleanest limb. |
| **(a)(4)** | Intent to defraud, furthering the fraud, and **obtaining anything of value**, with a carve-out where the only thing obtained is computer use worth $5,000 or less in a year | **Structural, not absolute.** Florida reaches fraud too, at 815.06(3)(b)2, but as an **enhancement** to the base access offense rather than as its own offense, and with no equivalent carve-out. |
| **(a)(5)** | **Damage** as the offense itself, across three mental states, with "damage" and "loss" statutorily defined | **Structural.** Florida reaches damage at 815.06(3)(b)1 but only once it **reaches $5,000**. Federal (a)(5)(A) has no dollar floor. |

**The honest reading.** One limb is absolutely distinct and two are structurally
distinct. Florida turns fraud and damage into enhancements gated on a threshold
or a purpose; the federal statute makes each an offense in its own right with
its own elements. That is a real difference and it survives the rule, but it is
a subtler difference than the drug or gun pairs, and a writer who reaches for
"unauthorized access, but federal" will still collapse the pair.

**Write it as three offenses, not as one offense with a federal hook.** That is
the operative instruction this brief hands forward.

### Determination

**Florida computer crime does not belong in the white-collar silo as currently
labelled.** The white-collar limb is fraud, which Florida handles as an
enhancement rather than as the offense, and which the federal page already
carries at 1030(a)(4). What is left in the Florida page after the fraud limb is
unauthorized access and damage, neither of which is a financial crime.

**RESOLVED 2026-08-13 as DECISIONS.md D14: option 1 was taken.** The page moved
to `/criminal-defense/computer-crimes-defense`, relabelled "Unauthorized
Computer Access". The options as put:

1. **Preferred: move it to the state silo** as `/criminal-defense/computer-crimes-defense`, built on F.S. 815.06 and 815.04, as a Florida offence page alongside the other state charges. The white-collar silo keeps no computer page, and the federal page carries the fraud limb.
2. **Alternative: keep it in white-collar but narrow the label** to the fraud limb, something like "Computer Fraud", built on 815.06(3)(b)2 and 815.04(4)(b). This is defensible but leaves the Florida access and damage offences with no page at all.
3. **Not recommended: leave it as is.** A page labelled "Computer Crime" in a white-collar silo will be written toward a SERP that is one-third CSAM defence, which is the wrong page in the wrong silo.

**A separate warning that falls out of the same evidence.** The `/internet
crimes/` phrasing in this market pulls child pornography defence at position 7.
Whichever page is built, it must not drift into CSAM territory, which has its
own page at `/federal-criminal-defense/csam-defense` and its own sensitive-topic
framing requirements under plan Section 12.

## 4. The question set a person would ask an AI assistant

1. "What is the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act?"
2. "Is it a federal crime to log into an account I am not supposed to use?"
3. "What does it mean to exceed authorized access?"
4. "What counts as damage under the CFAA?"
5. "Is my computer case state or federal?"
6. "What if I did not take anything, I just looked?"
7. "How much time does a computer fraud charge carry?"
8. "What makes a computer a protected computer?"

Questions 1, 4, and 7 are the FAQ block. Question 6 is the sharpest one on the
list: it separates (a)(4), which requires obtaining something of value, from
(a)(5), which requires damage, from mere access. A reader who did neither is in
a different position under each subsection, and no page in this SERP explains
that.

## 7. Sources and verification status

### Verified this session, fetched 2026-08-13

**https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title18-section1030**
(allowlisted per D2). Current 2024 Main Ed. (1/6/2025).

> **(a)(4):** "knowingly and with intent to defraud, accesses a protected computer without authorization, or exceeds authorized access, and by means of such conduct furthers the intended fraud and obtains anything of value, unless the object of the fraud and the thing obtained consists only of the use of the computer and the value of such use is not more than $5,000 in any 1-year period"

> **(a)(5):** "(A) knowingly causes the transmission of a program, information, code, or command, and as a result of such conduct, intentionally causes damage without authorization, to a protected computer; (B) intentionally accesses a protected computer without authorization, and as a result of such conduct, recklessly causes damage; or (C) intentionally accesses a protected computer without authorization, and as a result of such conduct, causes damage and loss."

> **(a)(1):** "having knowingly accessed a computer without authorization or exceeding authorized access, and by means of such conduct having obtained information that has been determined by the United States Government pursuant to an Executive order or statute to require protection against unauthorized disclosure for reasons of national defense or foreign relations, or any restricted data, as defined in paragraph y. of section 11 of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954, with reason to believe that such information so obtained could be used to the injury of the United States, or to the advantage of any foreign nation willfully communicates, delivers, transmits, or causes to be communicated, delivered, or transmitted ... the same to any person not entitled to receive it, or willfully retains the same and fails to deliver it to the officer or employee of the United States entitled to receive it"

> **(e) "damage":** "any impairment to the integrity or availability of data, a program, a system, or information"

> **(e) "loss":** "any reasonable cost to any victim, including the cost of responding to an offense, conducting a damage assessment, and restoring the data, program, system, or information to its condition prior to the offense, and any revenue lost, cost incurred, or other consequential damages incurred because of interruption of service"

Penalties under subsection (c), verified: (a)(4) carries a maximum of five years
on a first offense and ten with a prior conviction under the section; (a)(5)(A)
carries ten years on a first offense and twenty with a prior.

The definition of "protected computer" at 1030(e)(2) is quoted in
docs/build/OVERLAP-AUDIT.md and is not repeated here.

**https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/815.06** (allowlisted). 2025
Florida Statutes. Fetched for the comparison and the placement question.
> (3)(a) base offense: "a felony of the third degree"
> (3)(b) second degree felony where the violation: "1. Damages a computer, computer equipment or supplies, a computer system, or a computer network and the damage or loss is at least $5,000; 2. Commits the offense for the purpose of devising or executing any scheme or artifice to defraud or obtain property; 3. Interrupts or impairs a governmental operation or public communication, transportation, or supply of water, gas, or other public service; or 4. Intentionally interrupts the transmittal of data to or from, or gains unauthorized access to, a computer ... belonging to any mode of public or private transit"
> (3)(c) first degree felony where the violation: "1. Endangers human life; or 2. Disrupts a computer, computer system, computer network, or electronic device that affects medical equipment used in the direct administration of medical care or treatment to a person."
